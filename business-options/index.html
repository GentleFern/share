Your own small business: some options

Text too small? Make it bigger

You've been thinking about four kinds of work you could do on your own: selling your baking, making videos of your dogs, earning from Amazon links, and selling used books. This page gives a short look at each so you can compare them, then what each could pay if you get good at it and how they fit together.

None of it is a plan you have to follow. You could pick one, try several, or set them all aside, and change your mind as you go. None needs a big investment you could lose.

After Year 2

There are no dollar figures past Year 2 on purpose. The evidence there is too thin to put numbers on. What can be said is what keeps building, what levels off, and what you'd get to decide. By then, your own Year 1 and Year 2 results will be better evidence than any survey.

Home baking

Keeps building: repeat customers, word of mouth, and the prices your reputation supports.

Levels off: once your hours are full. Working alone, that's somewhere around $25k–40k a year in sales.

Your choice: stay at a size you enjoy, take on a helper, or move to a licensed kitchen ($3k–7k a year) to sell wholesale. A storefront is a different business. About 55% of new food businesses are still open after five years.

Weak evidence, but the limits are known

Videos

Keeps building: older how-to videos that people find through search keep bringing in views for years, along with customers for anything else you sell.

Levels off: no one can say. Platforms changed their pay rules four times in 2026, and studies that track creators over several years only follow the ones who stayed.

Your choice: keep it for fun, use it to promote anything else you sell, or treat it as a second income if Year 2 shows real growth.

Speculative

Amazon links

Keeps building: links in older videos keep earning while the products are still sold.

Levels off: it moves up or down with your video audience, and with Amazon's rates. Amazon cut those rates in 2020 and again in 2026.

Your choice: keep it as a small extra, and don't plan around it.

Speculative

Used books

Keeps building: your knowledge, which raises what you earn per hour up to a point.

Levels off: income doesn't build on itself. Year 3 looks like Year 2 unless you add hours, and the lifting gets harder over time.

Your choice: keep it if you enjoy the hunt, narrow to a specialty, or move those hours to something else.

Weak evidence
How these ranges were built, and what they can't tell you
  • Who they describe: people who keep at it for at least two years, improve, price sensibly and adjust from their own numbers. They are not the averages across everyone who tries.
  • Top 50% to 10%: built from the four research reports' cost and income models (home baking, dog social media, Amazon affiliate, used books) plus population anchors. Examples: 95% of Minnesota cottage producers expect under $7,665 a year in sales, and 30.8% of small YouTube channels with 52+ uploads crossed 1,000 subscribers in a year.
  • Top 5% and 1%: no dataset measures these groups. They mark the edge of what the models allow (your hours, the $78k cottage cap, platform pay rates), checked against the few upper-tail cases on record. For the videos, the top 1% depends mostly on luck.
  • Year 1 is your first full year once you start in earnest, with a few hours a day from the start. That makes it a ramp-up year. Year 2 is the year after. All figures are before income and self-employment tax.
  • Full reasoning and sources: the full write-up. New sources: BLS business survival, AIR Media-Tech YouTube pay by niche, Homegrown wholesale pricing, Escoffier bakery costs.

How they fit together

What the figures above don't show: how the options can help each other, the dates that matter, when to stop, and a few ways it could go. None of it is a plan you have to follow.

How they help each other
  • Dogs and baking. Videos and photos of your dogs with your finished baking, and a small line of dog treats, connect the two. The same home-baking registration covers baked dog treats, and they're the only home-made food you may mail before August 2027.
  • A simple video format. Show finished treats with the dogs in the living room or yard. MDA's rules keep pets out of the kitchen while you bake to sell, so bake on camera only for practice batches you won't sell.
  • Dogs and Amazon. Amazon links only earn once your videos have regular viewers. Then links to the harness, bed or toys your dogs actually use can earn small commissions.
  • Books stand alone. Specialist cookbooks may suit you, since you know baking, but the research didn't test them.
Dates worth knowing
  • Now, since you already take pre-orders: check that you're registered, that every package has the required label, and that your city allows pickup at home.
  • Before your first public sale: liability insurance, about $300 a year.
  • February 2027: YouTube's new rules start. New channels need 1,000 subscribers and 8,000 hours of viewing in a year before ads pay.
  • By April 1 each year: retake the free home-baking course and renew your registration.
  • August 1, 2027: Minnesota's new home-baking rules. Everyone pays $30 a year and takes a new course, and you may ship within Minnesota.
  • By September: apply to holiday fairs, as they fill early.
  • Once videos get regular views: consider Amazon links; the Amazon Influencer Program once you have about 1,000 followers.
A few ways it could go
  • Try two for a season, then choose. Give each a few months and compare them in a yearly review.
  • Focus on one. Pick whichever appeals most and leave the rest aside.
  • One main, others around it. For example, baking with dog videos that show off the treats, or videos with Amazon links once they have viewers.
  • Something else entirely. If none of these fits, that's a fine answer too.

Good rules for whichever you choose

A yearly review

Once a year, write down for each thing you're doing:

  1. Hours spent, money spent, money in, and free products received.
  2. Did I enjoy it?
  3. Is it growing: more repeat customers, more views, more sales?
  4. Keep, change, or stop?

Rules that apply to all four

  • Never pay for a business course or "system". Free training is enough. The only course you pay for is the state's home-baking course, from August 2027.
  • Take payment before you bake, and never refund an "overpayment". Real brands never ask creators for money.
  • Keep one folder for all of it. Receipts, a notebook of sales, a mileage log and a list of free products with their values. Take it to a tax preparer each January. Profit from everything together is taxable, and self-employment tax applies once it reaches $400 a year.
  • Before you claim Social Security: if you claim before your full retirement age, earnings above $25,560 in 2027 reduce your benefit. These ventures are unlikely to reach that, but check.
  • Say when you were paid or given something, in every video.

Option 1 of 4

Home baking

Growing the pre-orders you already take into a small business.

What it is. Selling cookies, bars, cupcakes and cakes you bake at home, legally, under Minnesota's "cottage food" law. You take a short free course and register with the Minnesota Department of Agriculture (MDA). There's no license and no routine kitchen inspection.

What it takes

  • Money: under about $200 to start, plus about $300 a year for liability insurance before you sell in public.
  • Time: as much as you choose. Orders come in bursts: holidays, graduations, Valentine's Day. January to March is slow.
  • Technology: very little. You can register on a paper form.

What it might bring. In the typical cases modelled, about $8 to $13 an hour before tax. Orders from people you know come to about $136 profit a month; a summer market or decorated-cookie orders can bring more, once you have the customers. Most home sellers sell very little: the typical one in a national survey made about $500 profit a year.

Does it grow? We expect it to, through repeat customers, word of mouth and photos of your work; the research doesn't measure how fast. Finding customers is the hard part, not the baking.

Might suit you if you enjoy baking for people and like setting your own prices and choosing your orders.

Think twice if deadlines, customers and labelling every package sound more like a job than a pleasure.

Worth checking now, since you already take pre-orders: that you're registered with MDA, that every package carries the required label ("These products are homemade and not subject to state inspection."), and that your city allows customers to pick up at your home.

A first small step: call city hall and ask about home pickup.

Option 2 of 4

Videos

Filming your dogs, your baking, both, or everyday life, and posting videos people can find.

What it is. Making YouTube videos and posting them. You could film your dogs, your baking, both, or everyday life; each has its own trade-offs (below), and you can change your mind. A family member can do the editing and uploading.

What it takes

  • Money: about $0. A $20 to $40 clip-on microphone is optional.
  • Time: about 3 to 5 hours a week for dog clips; baking videos take more, because each needs a planned bake, a clean, well-lit counter and shots of each step.
  • Technology: your phone for filming. The editing app and account setup are what a helper covers.

What it might bring. Most new channels earn nothing to a few hundred dollars in their first year. YouTube pays ad money only once a channel reaches 1,000 subscribers and 8,000 hours of viewing in a year. Of small channels that post every week for a year, about three in ten get past 1,000 subscribers. Once a channel qualifies, YouTube pays roughly $1.50 to $3 per 1,000 views for most topics, food included. The first money usually comes from free products, small paid posts, and links to products you use.

Does it grow? It can keep earning from past work. People search for recipes, often at the same season every year, so a good baking video can keep being found; dog clips mostly get their views in the first days. It's a long-term bet: judge it after a year, not a month.

What you could film

  • Your dogs: the least work, since you film ordinary life. People find these mostly through the app's feed, and soon after posting. Extra income: pet brands, or licensing a clip.
  • Your baking: the most work, since each video is a planned bake. People search for recipes, often every year at the same season. Extra income: kitchen product links, a recipe blog, and local customers.
  • Baking, with the dogs as a cast: two setups, since pets stay out of the kitchen while you bake to sell. Found both ways, with both kinds of sponsor.
  • Everyday life: medium work. Found by subscribers and the feed, rarely by search, so it's the hardest to grow.

Baking videos could also bring local customers to your pre-orders, even if the channel never earns ad money. A few people have built big cooking channels starting in their sixties, usually with family filming and editing, but they are rare.

Might suit you if you'd enjoy being on camera or behind it, and would like something creative to learn.

Think twice if filming would feel like a chore, or you'd rather not have your voice, home or dogs online.

Watch out for: anyone charging you to get started, and "brand ambassador" messages that ask for money or bank details. Keep your home private, and stop filming at the first sign a dog is stressed.

A first small step: take phone clips of whatever you'd enjoy filming for a few weeks, and see whether you like it. It costs nothing.

Option 3 of 4

Amazon links

Small commissions on products you show in your videos.

What it is. When someone buys a product through a link you share, such as the harness or bed your dogs really use, Amazon pays you a small share. Later, Amazon's Influencer Program can show your short product videos on Amazon's own product pages.

What it takes

  • Money: $0 to join.
  • Time: a little per video, once the videos exist.
  • Technology: a computer to make links, and a helper for one sitting to sign up.
  • An audience first. Amazon closes your account if you don't make three sales within 180 days of joining, so videos come first.

What it might bring. Near $0 in the first year. After a year or two of steady videos, ten to a few hundred dollars a month is plausible. Pet products pay 3%, so a typical $45 order earns about $1.35.

Does it grow? Slowly, and only while you keep posting videos people watch. It's an add-on to videos, not a business on its own.

Might suit you if you're already making videos and like recommending things you use.

Think twice if you aren't doing videos; on its own it earns nothing.

Watch out for: "five-star review for a refund" offers, which break Amazon's rules, and fake "Amazon support" messages asking for your password. Free products count as taxable income.

A first small step: none yet. If you make videos, mention the products your dogs really use, and look at joining around month 6.

Option 4 of 4

Used books

A treasure hunt that pays for the hours you put in.

What it is. Buying books cheaply at thrift stores, library sales and yard sales, and selling the few worth more online, mostly on eBay. Most books aren't worth reselling; the money is in textbooks, specialist nonfiction and older out-of-print titles.

What it takes

  • Money: a few hundred dollars.
  • Time: about 7 hours a week in the typical case.
  • Other: a car, dry storage space, a smartphone, and a way to print shipping labels. Your first eBay listing is the hardest tech step; do it once with a helper.

What it might bring. The first three months usually earn less, because you buy books before they sell. After that, a typical month is about $220, or about $7 an hour; a good month about $760.

Does it grow? Your skill does, and a book can keep selling for weeks or months after you list it. But the income stops when you stop restocking.

Might suit you if you'd enjoy the hunt at $5 to $10 an hour, with a chance of more as you learn. Since you know baking, specialist cookbooks might suit you, though the research didn't test them.

Think twice if heavy boxes, a record for every book, and trips to the post office don't appeal.

A first small step: list five of your own books on eBay with a helper. Or scan 200 books at one thrift store with the free BookScouter app, note which would have made $5, and buy nothing.

Selling your baking from home in Minnesota: a beginner's guide

For you if you already bake cupcakes and cookies well, take some pre-orders, and live in Minnesota, and want to grow that into your own small business over the next year or two. Figures and rules are as of October 2026. The law changes on August 1, 2027, so check the Minnesota Department of Agriculture's (MDA's) cottage food page again in spring 2027.

In one minute
  • Minnesota lets you sell home baking legally under its "cottage food" law, with a short course and a registration, and without a license or routine kitchen inspection.
  • Today it's free if you sell $7,665 or less a year. From August 2027 everyone pays $30 a year and takes a new course of up to eight hours every three years, and you'll be allowed to ship within Minnesota.
  • Most cookies, bars, cupcakes and cakes are allowed. Anything that needs the fridge to stay safe isn't: whipped cream, custard fillings, cheesecake, fresh-fruit toppings.
  • It builds on what you already do. It uses a skill you already have, needs little technology, starts for under about $200, and doesn't depend on an app's rules.
  • It pays like a small business, not a wage: about $8 to $13 an hour in typical cases, less in a slow week. We expect it to grow as customers come back and tell friends; the research doesn't measure how fast.
  • Finding customers is the hard part, not the baking.
  • Keep the dogs and cats out of the kitchen while you bake; MDA's rules require it.
What to expect

Most home bakers sell very little. In a 2016 national survey, the typical registered home food seller made $2,000 of sales and $500 of profit a year, working about 15 hours a week. About 95% of Minnesota's registered sellers are in the free small tier. A skilled baker with friends, coworkers and a church to sell to should do better than that, but more Minnesotans register every year.

These monthly figures are modelled, before income and self-employment tax:

How you sellTypical monthHoursPer hour
Orders from people you know (now, while working)about $136 profit14about $10

Decorated-cookie custom orders (after you retire)about $765 profit60about $13

A bad month at a market can pay under $1 an hour; a good month of decorated orders about $17. Orders pay best. A market's real value in the first season is finding customers who'll order later. January to March is slow; graduations, holidays and Valentine's Day are busy.

None of these is a wage; a typical case pays less than the $15.81 median cashier wage. What you get instead is control: you set prices, choose orders and own your customer list. The decorated-order model needs a reputation, which in our view comes from word of mouth and photos of your work. Decorated cookies were the top seller by volume in a 2021 survey of home bakers.

Plan for the typical row, not the good one. People starting a small business usually expect to beat the odds.

What you need
  • Your kitchen, with a way to keep pets out (a door or baby gate).
  • Your Social Security number, for the registration.
  • Recipes from tested sources, kept in a binder.
  • Labels: a word processor template or pre-printed stickers, and a date stamp.
  • Boxes or bags. Not napkins, forks or plates, because those make your baking taxable.
  • Cash for change, and later a payment app if you want one.
  • About $300 a year for liability insurance, before you sell in public.
Before you start: five things
  1. Call your city hall. The state law doesn't override local zoning. Ask whether customers may pick up at your home.
  2. Check every recipe against the allowed list (below). Drop whipped cream, custard, cheesecake and fresh-fruit toppings, and use cream cheese frosting only in a tested high-sugar recipe.
  3. Take payment before you bake. Ask at least half up front, non-refundable, for large orders. Never accept a check from a stranger, and never refund an "overpayment"; that's a common scam.
  4. Hand everything over in person until August 2027: at your door, at a market, or delivered by you. No shipping, no delivery apps.
  5. Don't buy a cookie-business course. The free training from MDA, the Minnesota Farmers' Market Association and University of Minnesota Extension is enough.
Step by step

Step 1. Register, if you haven't already (autumn 2026). If you already sell pre-orders, check that you're registered with MDA and that your labels follow Step 2; if not, this is the first thing to fix.

  1. Take the free Tier 1 course and its 20-question test online from MDA, or join a free class over Zoom run by the Minnesota Farmers' Market Association, which includes the test and application.
  2. Apply online, or ask for a paper form by emailing MDA.Licensing@state.mn.us or calling 651-201-6062. You don't need to use the website.
  3. Wait for your card, about 3 to 4 weeks. You may start selling once your name shows in MDA's online lookup, which can take up to 30 days. Carry the card when you sell.
  4. Each year, retake the free course and test, then renew by April 1.

Step 2. Make your labels. Every package needs:

  • your name or business name;
  • your registration number (use this instead of your address, so your address isn't on every box);
  • the date you made it;
  • the ingredients, with any of the nine major allergens: milk, eggs, wheat, soy, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, sesame;
  • the sentence: "These products are homemade and not subject to state inspection."

Put the same sentence on a sign wherever you sell.

Step 3. Price your three or four best products. Add up ingredients and packaging per dozen, then your time. Plain cookies cost about $3 to $5.50 a dozen in materials, frosted cupcakes $7 to $9, decorated sugar cookies $5 to $9. Suggested Minnesota prices: plain cookies $12 to $24 a dozen, simply frosted cupcakes $24 to $36, themed cupcakes $36 to $48, decorated cookies $40 to $60. Small towns sit at the low end, Twin Cities suburbs at the high end. In one survey, plain cookies tied for the best profit per item, and cupcakes faced the most competition; decorated cookies bring the most per dozen but take hours.

Step 4. Grow the pre-orders you already take (holidays 2026 to spring 2027). Cookie trays, birthdays, graduations, showers. Our suggestion: ask happy customers to tell friends, and photograph your best work. Set a minimum order, take payment first and write every order in a notebook.

Step 5. Before your first public sale (spring 2027). Buy product liability insurance, about $300 a year. Your homeowner's policy almost certainly doesn't cover a home business, so tell your agent about the baking, and about customers coming to a house with four dogs. Apply early to one farmers market or holiday fair; fairs fill by early autumn. Markets will ask you to sign Form ST19 saying you sell nothing taxable.

Step 6. After you retire (summer 2027). If you want more customers, try one weekly market, mainly to meet people who'll order later. If sales will pass $7,665 before August, move up to Tier 2 (a $50 fee plus a $50 course). From August 1, 2027, register under the single $30 tier and take the new course.

Step 7. Decide by your own numbers (autumn 2027). Focus on whatever paid best and you enjoyed most: decorated-cookie orders, a market, cookies shipped within Minnesota, or a dog-treat line.

Your kitchen, your dogs and cats

MDA's training says: "Your Pets Cannot Help Make the Treats." Keep pets out of the kitchen while you bake, and clean and sanitize the counters if one gets in. A routine that works:

  • door closed or a baby gate up on baking days;
  • pet bowls, beds and litter boxes out of the kitchen;
  • ingredients and packaging in sealed bins;
  • counters sanitized before you start; apron, hair covered, lint roller;
  • wash your hands for 20 seconds, even with gloves;
  • no baking if anyone at home has been sick to their stomach in the last 72 hours.

Pet hair in a cookie is the most likely complaint, and the easiest to prevent.

A treat line for your dogs' fans

The same registration covers baked or dehydrated dog and cat treats, with the same label sentence. They're the only home-made food you may mail today; buyers in other states may bring that state's pet-food rules into play. Never use chocolate, xylitol, onions, garlic, grapes or raisins, macadamia nuts, hops, hemp or CBD. MDA's training also bans animal-based ingredients; egg-free recipes avoid any question. Bake them on separate days from people food. Your dogs make a believable story and photo subject, which links nicely with the dog-video idea.

Sales tax, income tax and Social Security
  • Sales tax: plain cookies, bars, cupcakes and cakes in a box or bag are exempt. If everything you sell is exempt, you don't need a sales-tax account. Napkins, forks or plates make the sale taxable, and so do candy and decorations sold alone.
  • Income tax: treat it as a small business from the first sale. Keep one envelope or folder for receipts, a notebook of sales, and a mileage log, and take it all to a tax preparer in January. Self-employment tax (15.3% on most of your profit, roughly 14% overall) applies once profit reaches $400 a year.
  • Social Security: if you claim before your full retirement age, earnings above $25,560 in 2027 reduce your benefit; the typical cases here are well below that.

Videos of your dogs: a beginner's guide

This guide is for you if you have dogs, a phone and a few spare hours a week, and wonder whether posting videos of them could bring in money. It is written for you, in the United States, in late 2026. Platform rules change often, so check anything marked as a rule on the platform's own help page before relying on it.

In one minute
  • Treat it as a hobby that might pay for itself. Most new accounts earn nothing to a few hundred dollars in their first year. A random sample of YouTube found the typical channel has 61 subscribers and the typical video 35 views.
  • Your edge is real dogs and your own voice. Platforms reward original footage that holds attention and that people share. Four dogs with distinct personalities are a ready-made "cast".
  • For videos that keep earning, use YouTube and make some longer videos. Long videos can be found by search and can keep getting views for years.
  • Start on one platform, with one free editing app. Add others later only if you enjoy it.
  • The money comes first from small things: free products from brands, small paid posts, and links to products you already use. Platform payouts need large audiences.
  • Protect the dogs first. Film what they already do, and stop at the first sign of stress.
  • Say clearly when you were paid or given something, out loud and on screen.
  • Decide after six to twelve months whether it is worth your time, using your own numbers.
What you need
  • A smartphone with a camera.
  • A window with daylight; filming at the dogs' eye level.
  • One free editing app (see Step 2).
  • Optional: a $20 to $40 clip-on microphone for voiceovers.
  • A notebook for the log of products and payments.
  • A helper for one or two sittings: setting up the account, the editing app, and the privacy settings.
What to expect

These are modelled ranges, not promises. No one has measured what typical pet accounts earn, and most published figures come from people selling courses, tools or agency services.

Most accounts never reach 10,000 followers, so the middle row is already an optimistic case; it models an Instagram account pitching brands actively, and its figures are before tax. The table comes from the research's worked examples. For comparison, the federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour, and the median cashier earned $15.81 an hour in May 2025. If you need money you can count on, ordinary part-time work pays more.

What this hobby can offer instead: time with your dogs, something creative to learn, a schedule you control, and videos that can keep earning slowly for years. A fair way to think about it is "a hobby that might pay for itself, with a chance of more".

Success stories you will see online are almost all from the very top. A Yorkie with about 2.5 million followers reportedly earned about $35,000 in 2021; that is one dog out of millions of accounts. Treat any "how I grew my dog's account" story as one lucky draw, and often as an advertisement for a course.

Before you start: five things
  1. Never pay to get started. Real brands and platforms don't charge creators. Courses with income screenshots and pet-modeling agencies that charge registration or photo fees are the costliest traps. Buying followers or views is banned outright.
  2. Brand offers can be scams. Fake "brand ambassador" messages flatter you and then ask for bank details or a shipping fee. Look up any brand's contact details yourself before replying.
  3. Keep your home private. Turn off location on your phone's camera (ask your helper to show you where), don't show your house front, street signs or car plates, and post a little later rather than live.
  4. Free products count as income. Products a brand gives you for a post are generally taxable at their value. Keep a simple log from day one.
  5. If you receive Social Security before your full retirement age, or any disability or means-tested benefit, ask before you start. Small amounts are unlikely to affect retirement benefits, but disability and SSI rules are stricter.
Step by step

Step 1. Pick one platform (week 1). If you want videos that keep earning, choose YouTube (see "Videos that keep earning" below). If you mostly want fun and baking customers, Facebook is where many adults your age spend time, and Facebook Stars (small tips from viewers) open once you've had 500 followers for 30 days; each Star is worth one cent. To receive Stars or brand deals you need an account set up for creators; do this with a helper, because this guide's research didn't cover the setup steps. YouTube's videos can be found by search for years, and it is the simplest place for product links later. TikTok is popular with younger viewers, but only about 5% of adults 65 and over visit it daily, and its shopping features are best left until later.

Step 2. Learn one free editing app (weeks 1 to 3). Use YouTube Create or Instagram Edits (both free) or TikTok's built-in editor; Facebook's own editor wasn't reviewed. Learn one well. Export (save) one clean file and post that same file anywhere you like; there is no penalty for posting your own video on several platforms, but don't re-upload a download that carries another app's logo stamped on it (a watermark).

Step 3. Film the dogs as they already are (ongoing). Keep your phone handy for natural moments. Hold it upright, at the dog's eye level, facing window light. Add captions (on-screen words), because many people watch with the sound off. If your cats wander in, they're welcome when they choose to be; the same stress signs apply. Formats that suit a beginner include a recurring "cast" of your dogs, a voiceover in which you narrate or "speak" for a dog, a "day in the life" series, and stories about senior or rescue dogs.

Step 4. Post two to four videos a week, without stress (months 1 to 6). Daily posting isn't needed. On TikTok, posting more mostly buys more lottery tickets rather than more views per video. A realistic week of three to five hours looks like this:

  • capture clips during ordinary life;
  • one session of 1.5 to 2 hours to edit three or four short videos;
  • 30 minutes to write captions and post;
  • 30 to 60 minutes to answer comments and check one number.

Step 5. Watch the right numbers. In the first six months, look at views per video and how often people share, not followers or money. The first second matters most: open with the dog doing something, not with a title.

Step 6. Accept the small money as it comes. A suggested order: months 1 to 6, dog videos only, accepting gifted products when they're offered; around month 6, decide whether to add Amazon links.

  • Gifted products and small paid posts are possible at any size. Gifted-only deals are common at small sizes, and paid rates for accounts under 10,000 followers are quoted at about $100 to $300 a post. Most small accounts get few or no paid offers in a year; free products are far more common.
  • Links to products you use, such as Amazon links for the harness or bed your dogs actually use, pay a small commission (3% on most pet products). Put these links on YouTube; most Facebook groups ban them. The companion guide on Amazon links covers this.
  • Licensing a viral clip pays a share, but the license is usually exclusive, and the agency may then remove copies elsewhere, sometimes even your own re-uploads. Read the terms before signing.

Step 7. Review at six and twelve months. Compare the money and the free products against your hours. If you enjoy it, keep going. If it feels like a chore, stop or slow down. Keep copies of your original videos on your phone and in the cloud; they stay yours unless you signed an exclusive license.

Videos that keep earning

The hope behind many video makers' work is that out of many videos, a few become hits that keep earning for months. That pattern fits longer YouTube videos best, because people find them by search long after posting; the research expects them to last for years. Short clips depend more on the app's feed. A format that suits you is baking how-to videos of 10 to 15 minutes, with your dogs as the cast, which also advertise your pre-orders. Minnesota's cottage food rules keep pets out of the kitchen while you bake to sell (see the baking guide), so film the dogs outside the kitchen, or film practice batches you don't sell. That combination is this guide's suggestion; the research didn't test it. Cut short clips from the same footage to post as well.

The catch is that YouTube pays ad money only after a channel joins its Partner Program. From February 2027, new channels need 1,000 subscribers plus 8,000 hours of viewing in a year. Most channels never get there: the typical YouTube channel has 61 subscribers, and in one large dataset only about 3 in 10 small channels posting weekly for a year reached 1,000 subscribers, and fewer also reach the viewing hours; posting every two weeks likely does worse. Once a channel is in, long videos pay roughly $1 to $6 per 1,000 views by outside estimates, so a hit with 100,000 views might bring about $100 to $600 over its life. For a channel your size, short clips will almost certainly earn nothing from ads after February 2027. Before the channel qualifies, long videos can earn a little through Amazon links, once you qualify for those, and may bring in baking customers.

Our suggestion: one longer video every week or two, with a family member editing and uploading, judged after a year, not a month; the research expects slow returns for at least a year or two.

Protecting your dogs

Popular "funny" pet videos often show stressed animals. In one study of popular humorous dog and cat videos, 82% of those with a clear view showed stress signs, and the harmful formats got more engagement. Your rules:

  1. Film what the dog is already doing. Don't stage scenes or rescues.
  2. Learn the stress signs: whale eye, ears back, lip-licking or yawning out of context, a tucked or stiff tail, leaning away, freezing, trembling. Stop at the first one, and don't post that clip.
  3. No scares, pranks, "challenges", spicy or human foods, or forced tricks.
  4. Skip costumes. A bandana for a short time is fine.
  5. Keep sessions short and let the dog walk away.
  6. Never present breathing noises or health problems as cute.
  7. Only promote products you would give your dogs anyway; check foods and supplements with your vet.
  8. Watch older dogs for tiredness and pain.

These rules come from welfare organizations' advice and the research above. With four dogs, film one or two at a time, and let a dog who doesn't want to be filmed sit out.

Never repeat a brand's health claims, such as "cures anxiety" or "helps arthritis".

Telling viewers when you were paid

The rules of the FTC (the Federal Trade Commission, which polices advertising) apply to every account, however small.

  • Disclose any payment, commission or free product. Say it out loud near the start of the video and show it on screen. A note only in the description is not enough on YouTube.
  • Wording that works: "Ad", "Paid ad", "Gifted by [brand]", or "I earn a commission if you buy through my links". Wording that may not work: "#partner", "gifted" without the brand's name, or "affiliate link" alone.
  • If a dog "talks" in your captions, you are still the one who must disclose.
  • Be honest. Only praise products you have actually used. Mixed reviews are both legally safer and more believable.
Before you sign a brand deal

For small deals, a clear email is enough. Ask for payment on delivery or a deposit, agree how many rounds of changes are included, and keep the right to refuse any scene your dog is uncomfortable with. If a brand wants to use your video in its own ads, that is a separate use and should cost extra; never give permanent rights to your dogs' names or images. Talk to a lawyer before signing anything large or permanent. On any paid or gifted post, use only the app's "commercial" music or your own voice; popular songs can get the post taken down.

Amazon links and reviews: a beginner's guide

This guide explains how people earn small commissions by recommending products on Amazon, and which Amazon programs are worth a beginner's time. It is written for you, in the United States, in late 2026, and works best alongside the guide to videos of your dogs, because Amazon links need an audience to earn anything. Amazon changes its rules often, so check its own pages before relying on any rule here.

In one minute
  • Amazon Associates pays a small share of what people buy after clicking your link: 3% on pet products, so a typical $45 pet order earns about $1.35.
  • You need an audience first. Amazon closes your application if you don't make three sales from other people within 180 days of joining, so make videos first and join later.
  • The first year usually earns close to nothing. After a year or more of steady videos, ten to a few hundred dollars a month is plausible.
  • The Influencer Program is the better fit for video. Amazon can show your product videos on its own product pages and pay you when shoppers buy.
  • Amazon Vine is not income. It gives free products, which are taxed at their value.
  • Never join "review for a refund" groups. They break Amazon's rules and can cost you your account.
  • Say clearly that you earn from your links, every time.
What you need
  • Your dog videos, on YouTube ideally, with a regular audience.
  • A computer with a web browser, for making links; this guide's research didn't check whether the Amazon phone app can do it.
  • Your Social Security number or tax ID and bank details, for Amazon's tax form and payments.
  • A helper for one sitting to sign up and make your first links.

What it can offer: recommendations you'd make anyway can earn a little, and a good video can keep earning for years while the product is sold.

How the arithmetic works

Your monthly earnings are: people who watch or visit × the share who click × the share of clickers who buy × the average order × the commission rate. A worked example with middle-case guesses for a website:

Video viewers click far less often than website visitors: for 10,000 views of a longer YouTube video, where 1 to 3% click, the research's estimate is about $10 to $45, and short clips earn less again. Dog food and supplements may count as grocery or health products and pay only 1%, and pet prescriptions pay nothing.

Before you start: five things
  1. Joining is free. Anyone selling you a ready-made Amazon store, a "done-for-you" affiliate website or a passive-income system is a red flag; the FTC (the Federal Trade Commission, which polices advertising) has sued several such sellers for millions of dollars.
  2. Don't post reviews on Amazon in return for free products or refunds. Amazon bans it and sues the groups that organize it. In the research, these offers are the most likely scam a beginner will meet.
  3. Don't buy through your own links, and don't ask family to. Those purchases don't count and break the rules.
  4. Commissions and free products are taxable income, even if no tax form arrives.
  5. Protect your Amazon login. Fake "Amazon support" messages asking for your password or a sign-in code are common. Open notices only inside the Amazon app or website, and turn on two-factor sign-in (a code from an app as well as your password).
Step by step

Step 1. Make videos first (months 1 to 6). Follow the dog-video guide. While you film, mention products your dogs really use: their harness, bed, toys, brush. Videos showing how you solve a problem with a product, and honest "six months with this harness" reviews, suit a beginner best.

Step 2. Join Amazon Associates (Amazon's program for people who recommend products) around month 6, once your videos get regular views. Sign up on Amazon's Associates site with a helper. List your channel; it must have public videos of your own, with your own commentary, and Amazon checks. Then complete the tax interview, an online version of the W-9 tax form. Amazon pays by direct deposit about 60 days after the end of the month of the sale, once you reach $10. Your 180 days to make three sales start now.

Step 3. Make your links. While signed in to Amazon, use the SiteStripe bar (a strip Amazon shows to Associates) at the top of a product page to copy your link. The simplest place for links is your YouTube video description; most Facebook groups ban them. You can also use a link page you control. Amazon's short amzn.to links are allowed; other link shorteners and disguised links are not.

Step 4. Disclose every time.

  • In your profile, write Amazon's required sentence: "As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases."
  • Next to each link, add plain words such as "I earn a commission if you buy through this link."
  • In every video that mentions a link, say it out loud too.

Amazon's sentence satisfies Amazon but not necessarily the FTC, so use all three.

Step 5. Apply to the Influencer Program. We suggest applying once you've made your first few Associates sales. Connect your Facebook, YouTube, Instagram or TikTok account. Amazon publishes no follower minimum, but people report that approval under about 1,000 followers is rare; you can reapply after about 30 days. Once approved, you get a storefront page and can upload short product videos that Amazon may show on its product pages. Pet products pay 3% there, but since April 2026 only when a shopper buys exactly the product in your video, and Amazon decides where videos appear. Plan on about $1 to $5 a month per approved video.

Step 6. Check what works, then decide (month 12). Amazon's reports show clicks and sales. Set up a separate tracking ID (a label Amazon adds to your links) for each channel to see which one works. Compare the commissions with your hours.

What you must not do

Amazon's rules include:

  • No Amazon links in print, PDFs or ebooks.
  • No copying Amazon's star ratings or customer reviews. Don't quote prices; say "check the current price" instead.
  • No "click my link to help me" requests or other rewards for clicking.
  • No links in paid or boosted ads.
  • Links in email or text messages only to people who asked to receive them.

Break a rule and Amazon can close your account and keep unpaid commission.

Other programs worth knowing

Some pet companies pay a flat fee for each new customer instead of a percentage. Reported examples include Chewy at about $15 per new customer and fresh-food or pet-insurance companies at $20 to $60. One such sign-up can pay 10 to 40 times a typical Amazon pet sale, though fewer people sign up. These rates change often, so check them inside each program. Amazon's rules limit using another program for the same click, so read them before combining links in one post.

Amazon Vine

Vine is invitation-only; you can't apply. Members get free products to review, not money. Each item is taxed at Amazon's estimate of its value, so a $300 gadget you didn't need could cost roughly $36 to $80 in tax. Vine reviews can't contain your links. At best, Vine saves money on things you'd buy anyway.

Selling used books: a beginner's guide

For a beginner in Minnesota with a car, storage space, a few hours a day and a few hundred dollars. Figures are as of October 2026. Fees and rules change at least once a year, so check them again before relying on them.

In one minute

You buy used books cheaply at thrift stores, library sales and yard sales, and sell the few that are worth more online. Most books are not worth reselling. The money is in a minority of books, such as textbooks, specialist nonfiction and older out-of-print titles.

Expect it to pay like a low-wage, flexible job you can do on your own schedule. It is not a dependable income. After a few months of learning, a typical beginner earns about $5 to $7 an hour. With experience, good local sources and some luck, it can be $23 to $34 an hour.

Start by selling books you already own. That costs nothing and teaches you the whole process. Only then start buying books to resell.

What you need
  • A smartphone, for the free apps and for photos.
  • A way to print shipping labels, or help from someone who has a printer. Ask at your post office whether it can print a label from a code on your phone; this guide's research didn't check that.
  • A kitchen or postal scale, padded mailers or small boxes, and packing tape.
  • A cart or wagon, and a dry shelf indoors for stock.
  • A notebook or simple spreadsheet for records.
What to expect

Most people who sell online earn little, and earn it in only a few months of the year. In bank data from 2012 to 2018 covering millions of people who sold all kinds of goods on online platforms, about 7 in 10 had earnings in only one to three months of the year. Nobody publishes what part-time book sellers earn. The figures below come from careful arithmetic using real 2026 fees, together with guesses about how many good books a beginner finds.

A month in your first year (months 4 to 12)Selling on eBay, about 7 hours a week
Bad montha loss of about $33
Typical monthabout $220, or about $7 an hour
Good monthabout $760, or about $25 an hour

This table assumes you lean towards niche and older books, which fetch more on eBay. If you get ordinary Amazon-level prices instead, a typical month is nearer $3 to $4 an hour.

The first three months are usually worse, because you buy books before they sell. Expect to spend more than comes in at first. About a third of the books you buy will never sell at a useful price. A good month needs experience, good local sources and luck together. Any single improvement, such as better prices or buying more books, adds about $1 to $6 an hour; no single change gets you much past $13 an hour.

Ordinary part-time work pays more per hour than a typical month here. Book selling is worth doing if you would enjoy it at $5 to $10 an hour, with a chance of more as you learn. Its other rewards are the hunt itself, choosing your own hours, and a skill that grows over time.

Plan for the typical row, not the good one. People starting a small business usually rate their own chances well above other people's. Ask yourself what matters more to you: a steady paycheck, or flexible work you enjoy.

Before you start: five things you must not skip
  1. Keep one simple tax routine. Keep one folder. Photograph every receipt. Write each sale in a notebook. Log your miles. In January, take it all to a tax preparer. All income is taxable, even when no tax form arrives. If you buy books to resell, you will usually report it as a small business (on the tax form called Schedule C), which lets you deduct the books, fees, postage and mileage; ask a tax preparer once, early. If it counted as a hobby, you could be taxed on everything you take in, with nothing deducted. Self-employment tax, roughly 14% of your profit, applies once profit reaches $400 for the year.
  2. What to record. Each book's cost, purchase date, sale date and price, and the miles for each buying trip. The IRS rate is 76¢ a mile from July 2026.
  3. Before you claim Social Security, check how earnings affect it. If you claim before your full retirement age (the age at which you get your full benefit), Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 in 2026. A few hours a week of book selling is unlikely to reach that, but ask Social Security if you plan to claim early.
  4. Never pay for a course, a "done-for-you" store or a list of books to buy. These are the costliest traps for beginners. Free tools are enough.
  5. Protect your back and lungs. Keep boxes to about 25 pounds, use a cart or wagon, and don't buy musty or moldy books.
How it works, step by step

Each step teaches you what the next one needs. You can stop at any step having lost very little.

Step 1. Sell your own books (cost: about $0). List a few of your own books on eBay. You'll learn how to photograph a book, judge its condition honestly, set a price, ship it by Media Mail (the Post Office's cheap, slow rate for books) and handle a buyer. eBay is the easiest place to start because it has no monthly fee. As a new seller, eBay holds your money until the book arrives plus 2 to 31 days, until you have 10 sales and $150. Your first eBay listing is the hardest tech step in this guide: do it with a helper, in one sitting. You can also sell books to buyback sites, which pay less but take the work off your hands.

Step 2. Learn to spot books worth buying. Install BookScouter on your phone. It needs no seller account. Scan a book's barcode and it shows what buyback sites would pay. You can also look up a book's recent sold prices on eBay. Safest first rule: buy only if BookScouter shows a buyback price above what you'd pay. Then you can never lose money on that book.

Step 3. Sell the better books online. List them on eBay. When you want to sell on Amazon too, open a free Amazon seller account on the Individual plan; it includes identity checks, so do that with a helper too. The free Amazon Seller app then shows a book's price, how fast it sells and whether you're allowed to sell it. Keep your records from the first sale.

Step 4. Grow only if it's working. Later, if you're selling a lot and have room and energy, consider having Amazon store and ship your books (called FBA), specializing in one kind of book, or paying for Amazon's $39.99-a-month plan. Don't start there. FBA costs money every month whether books sell or not.

The buying rules
  • Buy only books that will leave you at least $5 after fees and postage. Ask for more profit from a book that sells slowly, because it ties up your money and shelf space longer.
  • On Amazon, check "allowed to list" in the app before you buy. New Amazon accounts are often blocked from selling many titles, especially textbooks from big publishers. Sell those on eBay or to buyback sites instead.
  • Prefer books that sell quickly until you know your market. One sale can make a slow book look fast for a few days. Look at the price history and how many other sellers there are, not just today's number. If there are more than about 10 other sellers, or Amazon itself is selling the book, that's a warning sign.
  • Books that usually sell: current-edition textbooks (especially nursing, medical, law and technical), programming and technical books, medical references, academic religion and study Bibles, crafting and quilting, homeschool materials, local history, and out-of-print specialist nonfiction.
  • Books to skip: mass-market paperbacks, recent bestsellers, book club editions, outdated textbook editions, damaged, water-stained, musty or heavily highlighted books, advance reading copies, and teacher's editions.
  • Grade honestly and conservatively. Say if a book has highlighting, library stamps, a missing access code or a smell. Honest grading wins sales and keeps your account healthy.
Where to find books
  • Library sales are the best-known source. Ask your library's Friends group for its sale dates. Members' preview days cost $5 to $20 to get in and draw professional dealers with scanners. General days usually charge $1 to $5 a book. Bag days ($5 to $6 a bag) are cheapest but picked over and heavy.
  • Thrift stores charge from 10¢ to a few dollars, though Goodwill often pulls valuable books out first to sell online itself.
  • Estate sales are good for specialist collections. Yard sales are cheap but slow.
  • Follow each sale's rules. Some library sales ban scanners. Put back the books you don't want, don't block the aisles, and don't sweep whole shelves into a box.

Nobody publishes how many good books you'll find per hour of looking. You can find out for your own area cheaply: scan 200 books at one thrift store, write down which ones would have made $5, and buy nothing.

Your first month: a plan with stopping points

Turn the plan into "when… I will…" commitments:

  • Week 1: When I have a free afternoon, I will list five of my own books on eBay, with a helper.
  • Week 2: When those sell, I will ship them within two days using eBay's labels.
  • Week 3: On Saturday morning, I will scan 200 books at one thrift store, write down which ones would have left $5, and buy nothing.
  • Week 4: I will spend no more than $40 on books that pass the buying rules, and list them.
  • Day 30: I will look at my record: how many hours I spent, what I spent and what came in. If I didn't enjoy it, or no books would have left $5, I'll stop. Stopping here costs almost nothing.
  • Day 90: If my books aren't selling, I will cut slow books' prices or sell them to buyback sites, and decide whether to continue.
